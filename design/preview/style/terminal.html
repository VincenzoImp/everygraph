Terminal

Everything the everygraph command prints, set on the terminal surface. Each transcript is shown three ways — with colour and Unicode, without colour, and in plain ASCII — because the first is an enhancement and the other two must be complete on their own.

One transcript, three ways

The three columns are generated from one source, so they cannot drift. The right edge of every specimen is column 80; the figure above each one is its longest line, measured by the page.

Colour and Unicode are detected independently, so a fourth combination exists — colour with ASCII symbols. It is the third column wearing the colours of the first, and is not drawn separately.

Colour

The command emits seven instructions and nothing else: reset, bold, faint, and four named colours. It never sets a background and never sends an exact colour value, so the user's terminal theme decides the hues. The swatches show what the documentation and a matching terminal theme use; the figure beside each is its measured contrast against the terminal background.

Symbols and their fallbacks

Nine characters outside ASCII and one sign, all present in common monospace fonts and one cell wide. No box-drawing characters: tables are aligned with spaces only. Status fallbacks are words, so a log can be searched for error: and warning:.

Progress, alignment and width

Three rules of construction that every transcript below follows.

Anatomy of a progress line

12345
1
Indent, 2 columns. Phases belong to the command above them.
2
Phase name, 13 columns. One lowercase word, left-aligned: the longest name plus two.
3
Bar, 16 cells. A cell fills when its sixteenth is complete. Whole blocks only — no partial blocks, brackets, spinners or colour. With 20 cells the longest line of map would be 82 columns.
4
Detail, up to 32 columns. While running: percent, rate, time left. When finished: the exact count of what was made.
5
Elapsed time, right-aligned to column 78. Units are written out on every line.

The three states: finished (bar recedes, numbers stay), running, waiting

The same, ASCII only

The glyph is still a choice: blocks as in cli.md (used on this page), squares as in api.md, or rules. All three are one cell wide in common fonts.

On a terminal the running line is redrawn in place a few times a second and the lines above it never move. When the output is not a terminal nothing is redrawn: each phase prints one line when it finishes, without a bar.

Column alignment

Labels and values: the ticks mark two left edges and one right edge

A table: text starts on a line, numbers end on one

  1. Two spaces of indent under a headline, four under a sub-heading. Nothing is indented with tabs.
  2. Labels are lowercase, dim and left-aligned; the values start at the longest label plus two.
  3. Text is left-aligned, numbers are right-aligned so that digits of the same weight sit under each other; a column of decimals shows the same number of decimals on every row.
  4. A header aligns like its column, is dim, and uses the column's real name, so it can be typed back.
  5. At least two spaces between columns; no rules, borders or zebra stripes.
  6. Numbers to read have thousands separators (1,830,397,917); numbers to copy do not (a line number, a port, --memory 6GB). In a terminal the thin space before a unit is an ordinary space.
  7. Durations are right-aligned and written as they are said: 38 s, 1 min 41 s. No zero padding.

Maximum line width

Nothing the command prints is longer than 80 columns, in any of the three renderings. A wider terminal changes nothing, so a transcript pasted into an issue looks the way it did on screen.

  1. Lines that are redrawn stop at column 78, because a progress line that wraps cannot be redrawn in place.
  2. A table with identifiers too long for the width shortens them in the middle (0x28c6…1d60) on a terminal, and never when piped.
  3. Machine output is exempt: tab-separated rows and JSON are as long as the data.
  4. In a terminal narrower than 80 columns a progress line gives up its bar first, then its detail; other lines are left to the terminal to wrap.

A 60-column terminal: the bars are gone, the numbers are not

Help

Headings are bold and nothing else is coloured, so help reads the same in a pager. Descriptions are sentences without a full stop, one line each.

From a file to a map

What it understood, what it will do and what that costs, then one line per phase, a result and the next command. The first specimen of this page is the finished ingest; here it is while running.

Questions and inspection

Results are tables without decoration; descriptions of a file or a machine are labels and values.

Refusals, errors and warnings

One sentence of what, the reason with its arithmetic, then the ways forward. The last line says what state things were left in.

Interruption and resume

Stopping is not an error and gets no symbol. The message says what was kept and what was not, and the same command continues.

For scripts

A plan without a run, and output meant for programs. Machine output has one rendering: there is no colour or symbol to take away.

On a light terminal, and on other people's

The same bytes on a light background. Nothing depends on the background being dark: no background is set, no white or black is named, and dim is an attribute of whatever the foreground is.

Everygraph's light terminal

Three default palettes that are not ours

The command only names colours; these are the hues three widespread defaults give them. Figures are contrast against each background; anything under 4.5 : 1 is marked. Solarized is low in contrast by design, for every program. The finding that is ours: the suggested command in blue is hard to read on both dark defaults.

The next step: three candidates

SPEC.md gives the suggested command the accent, and in a terminal the accent can only be the colour named blue. Measured on five palettes, blue fails on the two commonest dark defaults, cyan fails on Solarized Light, and bold in the default colour cannot fail. This page keeps blue, as specified, until that is decided.
