Data & map

How data looks in Everygraph: the colours that carry it, the map and each of its states, the small charts and the tables around it. Specified closely enough to build from. Every map, number and name here is invented.

1Categorical palette

Ten slots, --d1 to --d10, for things that have no order: categories, labels, regions. The values in tokens.css were chosen by eye. They are measured here, and they fail; a replacement is proposed next to them.

!
Measured result.

Method. Distance is ΔE: Euclidean distance in OKLab × 100. Colour blindness is simulated with Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009) at full severity, in linear RGB. Floors: 15 between any two colours for normal vision; 8 under protanopia and deuteranopia, where 6 to 8 is tolerable only when something other than colour also tells the two apart. Every figure on this page is computed in the page from the colours it is showing.

What the curated set still cannot do. Ten colours that share a hue across both themes, stay at 3 : 1 or more against the paper and keep a calm chroma cannot all be 8 apart for a red–green colour-blind reader: a search over that space tops out near 7.5. So the full ten are for charts and legends where a label sits next to the colour. Where any two colours can meet with no label between them, as on a map, use fewer:

Contrast against each surface

An outline or a dot needs 3 : 1 against what it sits on. Text never wears a data colour: labels stay in ink, with a coloured mark beside them.

What changed, slot by slot

More than ten categories

  1. Nine hues, then “other”. The nine largest categories take --d1 to --d9 in order of size. Everything else is drawn in --d10 and named “other”, with its total and the number of categories folded into it.
  2. Never a generated tenth hue, never a second pass through the palette, never lighter and darker versions of the same slot.
  3. Colour follows the category, not the rank. Once assigned in a session, a category keeps its slot when a filter changes the order.
  4. The reader can choose which categories get a hue; the default is by size.
  5. On the map, three hues at most for an attribute, plus “other” and “none”. Any two regions can touch there.

2Colouring regions

An open decision. A map has as many top-level regions as the graph has large communities; this sample has twelve. Should each have its own colour, or should a few colours be reused, as on a political map? The same map, both ways, both themes.

(a) One hue per region

What it costs. Colour looks like identity, and stops being one at the tenth region: here three regions already share the neutral. Ten colours on one canvas include pairs a colour-blind reader cannot separate, and nothing stops such a pair from being neighbours. A thirteenth region changes nothing visible. What it buys. In a small graph, “the green one” works in conversation, and a legend can list the regions by colour.

(b) Five colours, reused where regions do not touch

What it costs. Colour no longer names a region: two regions far apart can be the same violet, and a legend by colour is impossible. The name, the outline and the count carry identity. What it buys. Only --d1 to --d5 are ever on the canvas together, the five that are furthest apart, and no two regions that touch share one. It works unchanged for 12 regions or 1,200.

The evidence

The graph that is coloured: a line joins two regions that touch. Children take their top-level region’s colour.

i
Recommendation: (b). It is the only one of the two that does not depend on how many regions the graph has, and its weakest neighbouring pair is measured, not hoped for. Keep (a) as a setting for small graphs.

Read it as: of all pairs of regions that are neighbours on this map, how many have the very same colour, and how far apart are the two closest colours among the rest. Under (a) both depend on which regions happen to be neighbours, and regions past the ninth share the neutral; under (b) no neighbours ever share a colour and the weakest pair cannot fall below the worst pair of the region set. Four colours would do as well as five here and would raise that floor (section 1, row d1–d4).

3Sequential and diverging scales

For quantities. Sequential: one hue, light to dark on paper and dark to light on the dark canvas, so “more” is always “further from the background”. Diverging: two hues around a neutral midpoint, for values with a meaningful zero.

Ramps and their lightness steps

Under each swatch is its OKLCH lightness. The small plot is that lightness step by step: a straight line means equal steps, which is what makes equal differences in the data look equal. The curated ramps are built to a constant step; the token ramps are close but uneven, and the token diverging ramp on light has two steps next to its midpoint that are barely distinguishable.

A metric on the map: PageRank in fifths

Five classes, --s2 to --s6; the two ends of the ramp are kept back so the lowest class still stands off the canvas and the highest still takes a label. Fills carry the value at full strength, outlines go to the canvas colour, and each label picks light or dark ink from the fill under it. A region that is showing its children has no fill of its own.

A change on the map: second half of the year against the first

Seven classes around “no change”. The midpoint is neutral, close to the canvas on purpose: what did not change recedes.

Legend rules

  1. Stepped, never a smooth gradient. One block per class, 10 px high, 1 px apart. In the viewer the legend is a surface card in the top-left corner of the map; in a small view or an exported figure it is a strip under the map, above the caption, as here.
  2. Bounds under the edges between blocks, in JetBrains Mono; the title says what is measured and over what (“summed over members”).
  3. One line saying how classes were made (“percentile among regions of the same level”), because fifths at one level are not fifths at another.
  4. Diverging legends label the midpoint class in words and put the sign on every bound.
  5. The caption strip repeats the encoding in words, so an exported image explains itself.

Link widths

Width = 1 + 7 × √(weight ÷ heaviest link shown), in px. The heaviest link is always 8 px, and the panel gives every exact figure. Shown: links at 1%, 6%, 25%, 50% and 100% of the heaviest.

5Zoom levels

The same place at five depths, from the whole graph to single addresses inside “DeFi protocols › Lending”. What a level shows is decided by size on screen, not by the zoom number: a region opens when its radius on screen passes 84 px (60 px in these small panels).

How a parent hands over to its children

A cross-fade of 190 ms, started when the region crosses the threshold and not tied to the gesture, so it never flickers while you hold the zoom near the edge (it closes again only under 90% of the threshold). With reduced motion the switch is instant.

6States of the map

Each with the caption it carries. The caption is part of the state: it is where the map says what it is not showing.

7Labels

Labels are placed greedily by priority; one that would touch another is dropped, never shrunk, moved far or overlapped. The count is what every region has; a name is a bonus.

  1. Priority, highest first: the selection; the hovered thing; ends and hops of a path; the six heaviest link targets; top-level regions; named regions; then by size on screen.
  2. A region label must fit inside its circle. Tried in order: name and count; name on two lines and count; name alone; name on two lines; name cut with “…” at the end (from 26 px radius). If nothing fits and the region is top-level or selected, the name goes just above it.
  3. Unnamed regions show the count only, in mono, from 22 px radius. Smaller unnamed regions have no label: the outline is enough.
  4. Minimum size 10 px. Under 11 px radius nothing is labelled.
  5. Collisions: 3 px of clear space around every label (6 px around a plate). The loser is dropped and comes back when you zoom.
  6. Halo always: 3 px in the canvas colour under the text, so a link or an outline never cuts through a glyph. On a metric fill there is no halo; the ink switches instead.

8Small charts around the map

Charts here are ink on paper. A data colour appears in a chart only when the same colour is on the map for the same thing; the accent appears only on what can be dragged or pressed.

Axis, grid and tick style

  1. One axis line, the baseline: 1 px --line-strong. Grid lines 1 px --line, horizontal only, four or five at most.
  2. Tick labels in JetBrains Mono 11 px, --muted; units follow a thin space (“150 M”); powers of ten as 10³, 10⁶. Axis titles in Inter 12 px, --ink-2, sentence case.
  3. Marks are thin: bars with a 2 px gap on each side, lines 1.5 px, dots 1.6 to 2.5 px. No rounded bar ends, no gradients, no shadows.
  4. Emphasis by ink, not hue: what is inside the window is --ink, what is outside is --line-strong.
  5. The window is a selection, so it wears the accent: 1 px outline over an --accent-soft ground behind the bars, two 8 × 28 px handles.
  6. Every mark has the exact value on hover (a native title here; the popover component in the product), and the same numbers exist as a table.
  7. Logarithmic axes say so in the title or subtitle, show minor ticks at 2 to 9, and never start at zero.

9Tables of results

The standard result table. Exact numbers, right-aligned in mono so digits line up; the unit once, in the header; the sorted column marked in words and by weight; and a footer that says when the result was cut short.

  1. Numbers: JetBrains Mono, tabular, right-aligned, thousands separators, a fixed number of decimals per column. Text and identifiers are left-aligned.
  2. Units live in the header, in parentheses, never repeated in cells.
  3. Sorted column: header in --ink 600 with an arrow and aria-sort; its cells in 500. One sorted column at a time.
  4. Missing values say “none” in --muted; never an empty cell, never a zero that is not a zero.
  5. The footer is not optional when rows were cut: how many are shown, of how many, in what order, and the ways to get the rest.
  6. Selected row on --accent-soft; hover on --surface-2.

With a bar in the cell

For columns where rank matters at a glance. A 3 px bar under the number, right-aligned with it, scaled to the largest value in the column. The number stays the content.
